# Distributed Ledger — DLT

> **A shared history in which no single node is considered the source of truth. The protocol determines what counts as truth.**

## The picture

A conventional database: a bank's server says, “You have 100 rubles,” and that is true because the bank said so.

DLT: several participants keep protocol-defined copies or views of the ledger. None of them declares the truth alone—it is determined by shared rules used to verify the records relevant to them.

## How it works

In a globally replicated DLT, Alice transfers 10 coins to Bob.

1. Alice signs the operation and broadcasts it to the network.
2. Each validating node checks **for itself** that the signature is valid, the coins exist, and the operation is allowed.
3. The network agrees on the order of operations.
4. Each validating node updates its copy of the relevant ledger state.

The copies are not updated simultaneously, and that is normal. What matters is that honest nodes eventually arrive at the same result.

## How it differs from ordinary replication

Both systems have copies. The difference is what exactly is distributed:

| | Company replicas | DLT |
|---|---|---|
| Who decides what is true | The cluster owner | Protocol rules |
| Trust | Nodes under common control | Participants do not have to fully trust one another |
| Verification | Replicas usually accept records from the leader | Protocol-defined participants verify the records relevant to them |
| Failure model | Usually crash faults | Depends on the protocol; may include **Byzantine behavior** |

A formula worth remembering:

> **Replication under one administrator usually distributes storage and coordination under a crash-fault model.**
> **Many DLTs distribute verification among independent participants and tolerate some Byzantine behavior, but the exact trust and failure assumptions are protocol-specific.**

In Byzantine-resilient DLTs, the assumption that “a node may lie” gives rise to signatures, independent verification, and more expensive consensus. Other permissioned DLTs may rely on narrower assumptions—for example, known identities, a trusted service, or only crash-fault tolerance.

Do not confuse Byzantine resilience with distribution by itself: Raft and Paxos also distribute coordination among nodes, and ordinary databases such as etcd are built on them. The relevant distinction is the failure model: classic Raft and Paxos tolerate crashes, whereas Byzantine-fault-tolerant protocols are designed to tolerate participants that send false or conflicting messages.

Participants do not have to be anonymous: if the group is known and admission is restricted, the network is called permissioned. Its protocol defines who may submit, validate, order, or see each record; not every admitted participant must perform every role. Open participation is a separate property, covered in [Permissionless](005-permissionless.md).

## The cost

- data or commitments are duplicated among the nodes responsible for them;
- records and consensus messages must propagate to the relevant participants;
- nodes need time to reach agreement.

It is usually slower and more expensive than a conventional database. The price buys protocol-defined participants the ability to verify records without relying on one database owner. If that is unnecessary, a conventional database is simpler and more efficient.

## DLT ≠ blockchain

DLT is a class of solutions. A blockchain is one way to build one: records are packaged into blocks, and the blocks are linked by hashes.

Under the terminology used here, every blockchain is a DLT. Not every DLT is a blockchain.

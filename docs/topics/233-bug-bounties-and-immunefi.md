# Bug Bounties and Immunefi

> **A bug bounty publishes a legal and economic path for researchers to report exploitable flaws before attackers monetize them.**

## The contract around the contract

A serious program defines:

- exact contracts, repositories, and websites in scope;
- eligible impact and severity rules;
- maximum and minimum rewards;
- proof-of-concept requirements;
- prohibited testing methods;
- disclosure and response timelines;
- payment, KYC, and safe-harbor terms.

Immunefi hosts many Web3 programs and mediates reports under platform-wide and project-specific rules.

## Impact is the point

A report should show a reachable path from bug to in-scope harm. “This line looks wrong” is weaker than a local-fork proof demonstrating unauthorized minting or locked funds.

Researchers must not test by stealing from mainnet, attack users, or publicly disclose an unpatched issue. Platform rules commonly require local forks and controlled proofs.

## Incentive alignment

The reward should compete with the value, risk, and effort of exploiting or selling the vulnerability. A tiny cap on a billion-dollar critical risk weakens the whitehat path.

Projects need funded payouts, fast acknowledgement, technical triage, clear severity decisions, and a practiced patch process. A bounty page without response capacity is theater.

## Bounties complement audits

Audits are time-bounded reviews before or around release. A bounty invites continuous independent testing as code, integrations, and economic conditions change.

It still does not guarantee coverage. Researchers follow incentives and scope; unknown bugs remain unknown.

The healthy loop is: reproduce safely, report privately, confirm impact, patch and verify, pay fairly, then disclose under agreed rules.

3. Why is unauthorized mainnet exploitation not whitehat testing?
4. How does a bounty complement rather than replace an audit?

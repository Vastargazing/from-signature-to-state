# Inflationary and Deflationary Models

> **Supply inflation means units increase; supply deflation means units decrease. Neither term tells you what market price will do.**

## Measure the supply

For a period:

```text
net change = issuance - burn
inflation rate = net change / starting supply
```

Positive net change is supply inflation. Negative net change is supply deflation. Zero is a stable nominal supply for that period.

This is different from consumer-price inflation, which measures the changing purchasing power of money against goods and services.

## Common monetary models

A fixed-cap model issues toward a ceiling, as Bitcoin does under current rules.

A perpetual-issuance model continues creating units to pay security or fund activity, possibly at a declining percentage rate as supply grows.

A burn-offset model issues and burns simultaneously, so net supply depends on network use. Ethereum follows this pattern.

Rebasing tokens directly change balances or shares according to a policy. Their unit count can change without ordinary mint transfers to selected recipients.

## Supply is only half the market

A deflationary token can fall in price when demand falls faster than supply. An inflationary network can rise when adoption and demand grow faster than issuance.

```text
price emerges from supply and demand, not supply direction alone
```

Distribution matters too. Ten percent inflation paid proportionally to every holder differs economically from ten percent paid to insiders or validators. The same aggregate supply chart can hide very different transfers of ownership.

## Security has a cost

Low issuance sounds attractive to holders, but validators or miners need enough revenue to secure the chain. Burns may reduce supply while fees still impose real costs on users.

Good analysis asks who receives issuance, what action causes burn, whether the security budget is adequate, and how predictable policy changes are.

“Deflationary” is a mechanism description, not an investment thesis.

# Regulation and Taxation

> **A transaction can be permissionless at the protocol level and still create legal, reporting, and tax obligations for the people and businesses using it.**

## There is no global “crypto law”

Rules depend on jurisdiction, date, asset, activity, and role. Issuing a token, operating an exchange, writing open-source code, holding assets, and marketing an investment are different legal facts.

Regulators may classify or supervise parts of the system under securities, commodities, payments, banking, money-transmission, sanctions, consumer-protection, or data laws.

Calling a protocol decentralized does not decide the result. Authorities look at actual control, promotion, fees, governance, custody, and services.

## Tax follows events, not wallet withdrawals

Many tax systems care when value is received or disposed of, even if no fiat reaches a bank account. Possible reportable events can include:

- selling one asset for another;
- receiving mining, staking, airdrop, or employment income;
- spending tokens on goods or gas;
- earning protocol rewards;
- receiving assets through a fork.

Exact treatment varies. In the United States, for example, the IRS generally treats digital assets as property and requires records of basis, value, and dispositions. Other countries use different categories and timing rules.

## DeFi complicates accounting

A swap, LP deposit, receipt token, bridge transfer, liquidation, wrapped asset, or rebasing balance may have a different tax result depending on whether beneficial ownership changed.

The blockchain records token movements, not their legal meaning or fiat value. Users need records linking addresses, timestamps, transaction fees, cost basis, and market prices.

## The working rule

Do not learn regulation from token marketing or assume old guidance still applies. Identify the jurisdiction and activity, then check current primary guidance or a qualified professional.
